## Introduction

You catch the ball, drop your eyes to protect it and set off dribbling. By the time you look up, three defenders are in front of you and two teammates are wide open, unseen. It happens in almost every youth game, and it is not a lack of talent: it is a lack of game reading. Coaches call it court vision, and it is not a gift: you can train it.

## What reading the game means

Reading the game means deciding with information instead of reacting on impulse. Before you catch the ball, you see where your defender is, the space ahead and which teammate is open. Then the order is simple: shoot if you have a good shot, pass if a teammate is better placed, and dribble only to create an advantage.

Players with good reading are not the fastest or the flashiest: they look first and act second. Looking before you act is what separates a good pass from a turnover.

## When should you dribble, pass or shoot?

With the ball in your hands you always have three options. A simple way to choose is to answer three questions, in this order:

1. Do I have a good, balanced shot within my range? Then shoot.
2. No shot, but is a teammate in a better position? Then pass.
3. Neither? Then dribble, but with a purpose.

The dribble is the third option, not the first. It is there to create an advantage: to escape pressure, to open a passing lane, to draw a defender in. Dribbling head-down just to get away from the problem takes you straight into two or three defenders, the mistake we started with.

## Lift your head before you dribble

The habit that changes everything is simple: look before you put the ball on the floor. Many young players start dribbling the moment they receive it and spend the rest staring at the ground.

When you catch the ball, pause in the triple threat position: feet balanced, ball protected close to your body, eyes on the basket. From there you can shoot, pass or dribble, and your defender has to respect all three. Get into the habit of scanning the court before the ball reaches you, so you know where your teammates are.

## How do you train game reading?

Game reading is trained in real decision situations, not by dribbling around cones on your own. Small-sided games, a few players in a tight space, force you to read and decide every second. A rule often used in [basketball practice plans](https://www.basketballforcoaches.com/basketball-practice-plan/) is the no-dribble game: without that crutch, you only have time to play if you lift your head, find the space and pass.

In the daily life of a youth club like CDEFF, in Funchal, it is the coach who adjusts these games to each player's age and level, and who helps you see what you cannot see yet on court. Nobody learns to read the game alone: your teammates open the passing lanes, and it is with the team that every decision comes good.

## Conclusion

Lifting your head, reading the defender and choosing between shooting, passing or dribbling is not a talent reserved for a few. It is a skill you build game after game, and it is good for far more than basketball: reading a situation before you act stays with you for life. Next time you catch the ball, look first. The game gets a lot simpler.
