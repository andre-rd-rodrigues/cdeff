## Introduction

Sport helps you do better at school because moving your body regularly trains your brain for what school asks every day: paying attention, holding your concentration and organising your work. It won't do the studying for you, but it gets your head ready for it.

## What does exercise do to your focus and attention?

When you move, your body isn't the only thing working. Physical activity is consistently linked to better attention and better control of what scientists call executive function: focusing, ignoring distractions and holding information in your head while you solve a problem. That is exactly the set of skills you use in a maths test or when organising a project.

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128) itself lists cognition, and school performance, among the benefits of physical activity for children and young people.

[Expert reviews in sports science](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/10/640) find strong evidence that exercise helps attention, mental control and, in particular, mathematics. The effect on overall grades is more mixed. In other words, sport gives you better conditions to learn, but the studying is still down to you.

## The training routine that also organises your study

The biggest gift training gives you may not be physical. It's the routine. You have a fixed schedule, you have to be on time, and only a limited number of hours in the day. That forces you to plan, and planning is half the battle in studying.

Many young athletes make the same mistake: they leave everything until after training, get home tired late at night and try to study with nothing left in the tank. The ones who organise themselves well do the opposite.

1. Mark your training times at the start of the week.
2. Fit your study blocks into the days and hours that are left, before you get tired.
3. Use the dead time, the journey, the break, to review a little at a time.
4. Protect your sleep as if it were part of training, because it is.

Learning to organise your time around a commitment is a habit that stays for life, long after you stop competing.

## Sleep and movement to learn better

Sleep is where your brain files away what you learned during the day. A short night before a test undoes much of the studying you did. So an athlete who sleeps well isn't only recovering their muscles, they're locking in the material.

As for movement, the World Health Organization recommends an average of about 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity for children and young people, mostly aerobic, with muscle- and bone-strengthening exercise on at least three days a week. It also helps to cut down on still time in front of screens, which steals hours from both sleep and movement.

You don't need to be an elite athlete to gain this. A good share of that time can come from training at the club itself, and the rest from playing, walking or cycling.

## A note for parents

For a parent, the message is simple: sport and school are not at war. An active child tends to concentrate better, sleep better and come to their studies with a clearer head.

The right amount and type of exercise vary with a child's age, level and history. If there are questions about concentration, sleep or training load, the coach can help adjust it, and a doctor or health professional is the right person to answer any specific concern.

And nobody makes this path alone: at Clube Desportivo da Escola Francisco Franco (CDEFF), a team walks alongside each young person as they learn to look after their body, their rest and their attention.

## Conclusion

Sport is not the opposite of studying. It's one of the best supports a young person can have for it. It isn't magic: it's your body and your brain working on the same side.
