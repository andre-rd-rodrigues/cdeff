## Introduction

Fifteen minutes to the first whistle. Your stomach tightens, your hands go cold, and your head starts running through things that already happened and things that have not happened yet. If any of that sounds familiar, you are in very good company. Almost every young athlete knows this feeling, even the ones who look calm from the outside.

Pre-game nerves are your body's natural response to something that matters to you, and they do not have to hold you back. You can settle them with three simple habits: a slow breath where the exhale is longer than the inhale, an honest line you would say to a friend, and a short pre-game routine you repeat every time.

## Pre-game nerves: what happens in your body?

When you get nervous, your body gets ready for action. Your heart speeds up, your breathing shortens, your muscles tense. In small doses this helps: it sharpens your reflexes and your focus. It only becomes uncomfortable when the volume goes up too far.

In younger athletes, pre-game [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety) often shows up in the body rather than in words. It can show up as a stomach ache, a headache, tightness in the chest, no appetite, or a rough night's sleep before a match. Recognising those signals as nerves, not as being ill, already takes some of the fear out of them.

## Breathe slowly, from the belly

Your breath is the one part of this whole mechanism that you can control directly, and it is probably the most useful skill you can train.

Try a slow breath with your belly rising more than your chest. This kind of belly breathing is easy to learn:

1. Breathe in through your nose for about four seconds, calmly.
2. Hold for one or two seconds.
3. Breathe out through your mouth for six seconds, more slowly than you breathed in.
4. Repeat five to ten times.

Making the exhale longer than the inhale gently switches on the part of your nervous system that slows the body down. Do it during the warm-up, or sitting on the bench right before you go on. Half a minute is enough.

## Talk to yourself the way you would talk to a friend

What you say to yourself before a game shapes how you play. Lines like *"I am going to mess up"*, *"if I miss, everyone will see"* or *"I cannot let my coach down"* pile pressure on top of pressure.

Try the opposite. Think about what you would say to a teammate as nervous as you are. Probably something like *"do your best"*, *"focus on the next play"* or *"missing is part of it, keep going"*. Those sentences work for you too. A mistake in a match is information, not a verdict.

## Build a short pre-game routine

A simple routine, always the same, is a powerful way to settle the body. Three to five steps done in the same order before every match:

- Arrive early enough not to feel rushed.
- Listen to the same music, or sit quietly for a few minutes.
- Warm up with attention, not in a hurry.
- One slow breath before you step on to play.
- One simple thought for the match, like *"play with energy and enjoy it"*.

Over time, your body learns that this sequence is the moment to shift gear, and the nerves start to settle almost by habit.

## When do pre-game nerves become a real problem?

A note also for the parents reading along. Nerves that ease once the game starts are normal. What deserves attention is when other things start happening: avoiding training, sleeping badly for several nights, losing their appetite before every competition, crying often, or losing interest in the sport they used to love.

At that point, the answer is neither to push through nor to brush it off. Talk to the coach, who sees the athlete in context, and, if it helps, ask a sports psychologist or a doctor. Asking for help is not weakness.

The pressure a young athlete feels at home counts too. Children read the silence, the serious face on the drive home, or the insistence on winning. A simple *"I loved watching you play"* usually lands better than any post-match analysis.

## Conclusion

Learning to handle pre-game nerves is a skill just as real as dribbling or passing, and it carries over into school exams and the rest of life.
