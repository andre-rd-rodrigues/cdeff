## Introduction

Before a training session or a game, your body needs to be ready. Arriving five minutes early, tying your shoes and running straight into the drills is the most common habit, and it is also the one behind many of the injuries you see in youth basketball and futsal. A good warm-up decides how well the rest of the session will go.

## How warming up helps prevent injuries in basketball and futsal

A good warm-up before basketball or futsal prepares ankles, knees and hips for the sudden starts, jumps and changes of direction repeated throughout the whole session. Done regularly, a structured neuromuscular warm-up, meaning one that trains muscles and coordination together, reduces the injuries typical of these sports and teaches the body to brake, turn and land safely.

Many young players skip the warm-up to start playing sooner. That is exactly where the injuries that keep them out for weeks come from.

## How should you warm up before basketball or futsal training?

An effective warm-up for basketball and futsal usually follows five phases, in the right order, in a little over ten minutes:

1. Light aerobic work. Three to five minutes of easy jogging or skipping to raise body temperature and get the blood flowing.
2. Dynamic mobility. Ankle circles, hip openers, trunk rotations. Avoid long static stretches, which cool the muscle down again.
3. Muscle activation. Hip bridges, light squats, side lunges to wake up the glutes and the stabilisers around the knee.
4. Sport-specific movement. Controlled landings from soft jumps, changes of direction on two feet, short sprints, dribbles or passes at a low tempo.
5. Brief agility. Two or three short bursts on an agility ladder, or a couple of accelerations, to prime the nervous system.

The point is to prepare, not to tire you out. If you finish warm, ready and focused, it did its job.

## Common warm-up mistakes in youth teams

Three traps show up again and again in youth training:

- Arriving late and skipping the mobility phase. The fastest route to a sprained ankle.
- Long static stretches on a cold muscle reduce strength and do not prevent injuries before a session. Save them for the end of training.
- Warming the body but not the mind. A warm-up also serves to focus, read the drill and arrive at the first play already inside the session.

Working on [proprioception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proprioception), your body's sense of where its own parts are in space, through single-leg balance, slow landings and controlled changes of direction is one of the best investments you can make for young ankles and knees.

## The coach's role and the warm-up habit

At CDEFF, the Clube Desportivo da Escola Francisco Franco in Funchal, Madeira, the coach adjusts the warm-up to the age and level of each athlete, and corrects a poor landing pattern before it becomes an injury. A young athlete who learns, at 10 or 12, to prepare the body before any effort carries that habit into adult training for the rest of their life.

Nobody warms up alone. A team and a club go through that routine with you, before every session.

## Conclusion

Arrive earlier, respect the five phases, and save the long stretches for the end of training. If a persistent pain shows up, talk to the coach and, if needed, a sports doctor. A good warm-up is the first step of someone who wants to keep playing for many years.
